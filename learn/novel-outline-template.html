Novel Outline Template

Free templates to copy for a one-page outline, a chapter-by-chapter plan, scene cards, and a reverse outline, with a filled-in example.

9 min read

The short answer

A novel outline template needs five parts: a one-sentence premise, the ending, the four turning points that hold the book up (the opening, the commitment, the low point, and the climax), a chapter list with one line naming what changes in each chapter, and a list of open questions. For an 80,000 word novel, that fits on two to four pages.

Which outline template do you need?

Pick the template that answers the question you are stuck on. You rarely need all four, and never all at once.

Most outline templates online are one long form that asks for everything up front. That suits a few writers and stalls the rest. The four templates on this page are built to be used in sequence: the one-page outline first, the chapter plan when you are ready to draft, scene cards for the parts that will not settle, and the reverse outline once a draft exists. For the thinking behind each method, see how to outline a novel.

One-page novel outline template

The spine of the book on a single page. Fill in the premise and the ending before anything else, because every other field is measured against them. Leave a field blank rather than inventing something you do not believe yet.

One-page novel outline

TITLE (working):
Genre / target length:
--------------------------------------------
PREMISE (one sentence: who wants what,
what stands in the way, what it costs)

--------------------------------------------
PROTAGONIST
--------------------------------------------
Wants (concrete, with a deadline):
Needs (what they cannot see yet):
What stands in the way:
--------------------------------------------
THE ENDING (decide this first)
--------------------------------------------
What they choose:
What it costs them:
The state of the world afterwards:
--------------------------------------------
FOUR TURNING POINTS
--------------------------------------------
1. Opening (the world before it changes):
2. Commitment (about 25%, no going back):
3. Low point (about 75%, their approach fails):
4. Climax (about 90%, the final choice):
Midpoint, optional (about 50%, what is revealed):
--------------------------------------------
SUBPLOTS (one line each)
--------------------------------------------
A:
B:
--------------------------------------------
OPEN QUESTIONS (flag gaps, do not fill them)
--------------------------------------------
?
?

Why a deadline on the want? A want with a date attached (“before the lease runs out at midsummer”) gives every chapter a clock. A clock produces scenes, and it tells you when the book is over.

Chapter-by-chapter outline template

Copy the block once per chapter. The field that matters most is What changes: a decision, a revelation, a relationship shifting, a door closing. If you cannot name what changes in a chapter, it is either missing something or it belongs inside another chapter.

Chapter outline (one block per chapter)

CHAPTER [number]: [working title]
Point of view:
Where and when:
What happens (two or three lines):
What changes:
What the reader learns:
Ends on (the question that turns the page):
Target words:

If you prefer a spreadsheet, the same fields work as columns, one row per chapter. Paste this header row into the first line of a sheet:

Chapter grid (spreadsheet header)

Chapter	POV	Where / when	What happens	What changes	Reader learns	Ends on	Target words	Actual words

The columns are separated by tabs, so they land in separate cells when pasted into Google Sheets, Excel, or Numbers. The Actual words column turns the grid into a progress tracker while you draft.

What does a filled-in novel outline look like?

Here are both templates filled in for an invented novel: a bookbinder in a river city who is hired to rebind a dead mayor’s diary and finds pages cut from it. Notice how short the entries are. An outline is a set of instructions to your future self, not a draft of the book.

Example: one-page outline

TITLE: The Cut Pages
Genre / target length: Literary mystery, 80,000 words
--------------------------------------------
PREMISE
A bookbinder about to lose her workshop is paid to
rebind a dead mayor's diary, finds eleven pages cut
from it, and has to choose between the fee that
saves her and the truth that floods the city.
--------------------------------------------
PROTAGONIST: Odile Marsh
--------------------------------------------
Wants: To finish the commission and pay the lease
  before it runs out at midsummer.
Needs: To stop hiding behind her craft and speak.
In the way: The mayor's widow, the binders' guild,
  and her own habit of keeping quiet.
--------------------------------------------
THE ENDING
--------------------------------------------
Chooses: To read the missing pages aloud at the
  memorial instead of handing over the diary.
Costs her: The fee, the lease, and the guild seal.
Afterwards: The flood barrier is rebuilt. She works
  from a borrowed bench and takes an apprentice.
--------------------------------------------
FOUR TURNING POINTS
--------------------------------------------
1. Opening: Three months from eviction, she takes
   the widow's commission.
2. Commitment (ch 7): She finds the stubs of the cut
   pages, hides that she noticed, and starts looking.
3. Low point (ch 20): The widow finds out, cancels
   the commission, and reports her to the guild.
4. Climax (ch 24): The memorial. She reads.
Midpoint (ch 14): The pages turn up inside a city
   ledger. The mayor knew the barrier was unsound.
--------------------------------------------
SUBPLOTS
--------------------------------------------
A: Her brother works on the barrier crew.
B: A rival binder wants her premises.
--------------------------------------------
OPEN QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------
? Who cut the pages: the mayor, or the widow?
? How does she get into the archive in ch 12?

Example: first three chapters

CHAPTER 1: The Lease
Point of view: Odile
Where and when: Her workshop, early spring
What happens: The landlord's letter. The widow
  arrives with the diary and a generous fee.
What changes: She accepts a job she would usually
  refuse, because she needs the money.
What the reader learns: Midsummer is the deadline.
Ends on: The widow says, "Do not read it."
Target words: 3,000

CHAPTER 2: Sewing Stations
Point of view: Odile
Where and when: Workshop, that night
What happens: She takes the old binding apart and
  counts the gatherings. One is too thin.
What changes: The job stops being routine.
What the reader learns: She does read it.
Ends on: A knife mark along the spine.
Target words: 2,500

CHAPTER 3: The Barrier
Point of view: Odile
Where and when: The riverside, next morning
What happens: She brings her brother lunch at the
  barrier works. He jokes that it leaks.
What changes: The city's danger becomes personal.
What the reader learns: The barrier was the dead
  mayor's proudest project.
Ends on: Water in the footings, in a dry week.
Target words: 3,000

Why this works:

  • The premise names the choice. “The fee that saves her and the truth that floods the city” tells you what the climax has to be about before a single scene is planned.
  • The ending is decided, the middle is not. Chapters 4 to 6 and most of the second half are still open, and that is fine. The turning points hold them in place.
  • Every chapter changes something. Even chapter 3, a lunch by the river, ends with the reader knowing more than they did.
  • The gaps are flagged, not filled. “Who cut the pages” is a real question the writer will answer by drafting toward it.

Where should the turning points fall?

Three-act planning puts the commitment about a quarter of the way in, the midpoint halfway, the low point about three quarters of the way, and the climax near the end. These are rough positions to aim at, not rules, and many good novels land a few percent either side. Multiply your target word count by each percentage to find where the turn should land:

Turning point Position 60,000 words 80,000 words 100,000 words
Commitment25%15,00020,00025,000
Midpoint50%30,00040,00050,000
Low point75%45,00060,00075,000
Climax90%54,00072,00090,000

To turn words into chapters, divide by your chapter length. At 3,000 words a chapter, an 80,000 word novel has about 27 chapters (80,000 ÷ 3,000 = 26.7). The commitment lands around the end of chapter 7 (20,000 ÷ 3,000 = 6.7), the midpoint in chapter 14, the low point at the end of chapter 20, and the climax around chapter 24. That is where the example above places them. For typical chapter lengths by genre, see how long a chapter should be; for total length, see the novel length guide.

Scene card template

For the parts of a book that will not sit still: a multi-viewpoint middle, a mystery with clues to place, two timelines to braid. Write one card per scene, on index cards or in any tool that lets you drag cards around, and shuffle until the order feels right.

Scene card

SCENE: [a few words]
POV:
Goal (what the POV character wants in this scene):
Obstacle:
Outcome (yes, no, yes but, no and):
What changes:

The Outcome line is the useful one. A scene where the character simply gets what they want is usually a scene where the tension leaks out. “Yes, but” and “no, and” outcomes push the story into the next scene on their own.

Reverse outline template

Written after a draft, one line per chapter, describing what you actually wrote rather than what you planned. It is the fastest way to see a sagging middle, a subplot that vanishes for ten chapters, or two chapters doing the same job.

Reverse outline (one line per chapter)

Ch | Words | What actually happens | What changes | Problem?
1  |       |                       |              |
2  |       |                       |              |
3  |       |                       |              |

When it is done, read only the What changes column from top to bottom. If it reads like a story on its own, the structure is sound. Any row where that column is empty is a chapter to cut, merge, or rethink. Discovery writers can keep this running as they draft, one line after each session, as described in the outlining guide.

How do you fill in an outline without stalling?

A blank template invites perfectionism. Work in this order, and stop as soon as you know what to write tomorrow:

  1. Write the premise in one sentence. Who wants what, what stands in the way, and what it costs them. If you cannot, the story shape is not settled yet, and that is the thing to work on.
  2. Decide the ending. What your protagonist chooses, what it costs, and what state the world is left in. You can change it later.
  3. Place the four turning points. Use the table above to find the chapter each one lands in for your target length.
  4. List chapters with one line each. Fragments are fine. “She lies to her brother about the letter” is a complete entry.
  5. Fill the full chapter template for the first three chapters only. That is enough to start drafting without deciding anything major mid-scene.
  6. Flag the gaps and start writing. Put every unanswered question in the open questions list rather than inventing filler, then expand the next few chapter blocks as the draft reaches them.

The outline will be wrong in places. Update it forward from wherever the draft surprises you, and keep it somewhere you will actually open it. If you are planning more than one book, the series planning guide covers what to track across volumes, and the facts of your world belong in a story bible rather than the outline.

Questions writers ask about outline templates

Is this novel outline template free?
Yes. Every template on this page is free to copy and use for any project, with no sign-up. Use the Copy button or select the text, then paste it into a document, a spreadsheet, a notebook, or the writing app you already use.
What should a novel outline include?
At minimum: a one-sentence premise, the ending, the four turning points (the opening, the commitment, the low point, and the climax), and a list of chapters or scenes with one line naming what changes in each. Subplots and a list of open questions are worth adding. Settings, backstory, and world details belong in a story bible instead.
How detailed should a chapter outline be?
Detailed enough that you could start writing the chapter today, and no more. For most writers that is three to six short lines: what happens, what changes, and what the chapter ends on. If you are fixing dialogue in the outline, it is too detailed.
How many chapters should my outline have?
Divide your target word count by your typical chapter length. At 3,000 words a chapter, an 80,000 word novel has about 27 chapters; at 2,000 words, it has 40. Treat the number as a starting estimate, since chapters will merge and split as you draft.
Can I outline a novel in a spreadsheet?
Yes. A spreadsheet suits the chapter-by-chapter outline well, with one row per chapter and one column per field. Copy the chapter grid header on this page into the first row. Keep the one-page outline in a document, since a premise and an ending read better as sentences than as cells.
