How to Plan Your First Book Series

Choosing a shape, balancing the arc that spans books against the story inside each one, and what to seed in book one so book three still works.

12 min read

The short answer

To plan a book series, first choose its shape (closed arc, episodic, cyclical, or shared world), then write the one-sentence question the whole series answers. Give every book its own complete story with its own resolution, decide what book one must plant for later books to pay off, and keep a series-level story bible with a dated timeline. Plan the shape, not the scenes.

Choose the shape of your series

Before anything else, decide what kind of series this is, because the shape determines how much you need to settle in advance and how each book is allowed to end.

Shape How it works Plan in advance Watch out for
Closed arc One large story told across a fixed number of books, usually three. The series has a definite ending. A great deal. You need the ending, the midpoint, and what each book contributes. A sagging middle book that exists only to move pieces into place.
Episodic Each book is a complete, largely standalone story with recurring characters. Common in crime and cosy fiction. Little. The world, the cast, and the premise, but not the plots. Stasis. If nothing accumulates, readers stop caring by book four.
Cyclical Standalone books that also build a slow underlying arc, resolving after several entries. The premise, the long arc's destination, and roughly when it surfaces. Pacing. The long arc can be starved by the standalone plots.
Shared world Separate stories set in one world, often with different protagonists and different periods. The world, its rules, and its history. Plots can wait. Readers who loved book one's cast and do not want book two's.

For a first series, the closed trilogy and the episodic series are the safest choices, for opposite reasons. The trilogy is finite, so you can see the whole thing. The episodic series is forgiving, because a weak entry does not damage the structure around it.

A word about length. Three books is a good ceiling for a first series. Three maps naturally onto a beginning, a complication, and a resolution, and it is a commitment you can plausibly finish. Series that were planned as seven books and abandoned at two are extremely common, and the reason is almost never talent.

The two arcs you are writing at once

Every book in a series carries two stories. There is the story of this book, which starts on page one and finishes at the end. And there is the story of the series, which passes through this book on its way somewhere else. Series that do not satisfy usually have one of these crowding out the other.

The book arc is a complete novel. Its protagonist wants something, is prevented, and either gets it or does not. A reader who picks up book two by accident should still finish a story.

The series arc is the larger question: whether the war ends, whether she ever finds out who her mother was, whether this town can survive what it did. It advances in each book but resolves only once.

The practical implication is a ratio. In most working series, roughly seventy to eighty percent of a book belongs to its own arc and twenty to thirty percent to the series. When the series arc takes over, you get the classic middle-book problem: four hundred pages of positioning and no story. When the series arc disappears entirely, the books stop feeling connected and readers drift.

Write the series question down in one sentence and pin it somewhere. Then, for each book, write a second sentence: what does this book do to that question. If the answer is "nothing, but it is fun", you have an episodic series, which is fine. If the answer is "nothing" and you are writing a trilogy, you have a problem.

What to settle before you finish book one

You do not need to plan three books before writing one. You do need to settle a small number of things before book one is finished, because they are extremely expensive to add later.

How the series ends, roughly. Not scene by scene. But you should know what the final state of the world is and who is responsible for getting it there. Without this, book one plants nothing useful and book three arrives at an ending that was never prepared.

What each book is broadly about. A sentence each is enough. Book two is the one where the alliance breaks. That level.

The rules of the world. Fully, and in writing. Rules invented in book two that contradict book one are the most damaging continuity errors in series fiction, because you cannot fix the published book. Get the constraints written down before book one goes anywhere.

Your cast's ages and the timeline. Tedious, and it will save you enormously. Decide how much time passes between books before you write the sentence in book two that fixes it accidentally.

What you are deliberately leaving open. Keep the list. These are your seeds, and knowing which unresolved threads are intentional stops you tidying them away in a revision of book one.

Seeding: what to plant and what to leave open

Seeding is the craft of putting something in book one that book three needs, without book one seeming to strain toward it.

The principle: introduce, do not explain. A seed is a person who exists, a place that is mentioned, an object someone owns, a fact stated in passing and not pursued. It does not need weight. It needs to be there, so that when it matters later the reader has met it and feels the click of recognition rather than the thud of a new element arriving at the moment it becomes convenient.

Good seeds share three qualities. They function in the scene they appear in, so nothing feels planted. They are specific enough to be remembered. And they are not underlined, because a seed the reader is told to notice is not a seed, it is a promise, and promises get counted.

The most useful things to seed early are people (a minor character in book one is far cheaper than a major one introduced in book three), places, unexplained facts, and unresolved relationships. The last of those does more work than the rest combined, because a relationship left unfinished in book one gives book two somewhere to start.

Be honest about the ones you have not thought through. Every series carries a few threads the author planted without knowing what they meant. Log them in your story bible under open questions, and decide on purpose later rather than pretending you always knew.

How each book should end

The most common structural error in first series is the ending that settles nothing.

A cliffhanger where the book's own story is unresolved asks the reader to trust you with several more years of their attention before you have shown them you can finish anything. Some readers will follow. Many will not, and the ones who do not will not say so.

The pattern that works: close the book's story, leave the series question open. She gets the evidence she has been chasing all book (resolved), and it names someone she trusts (open). The reader puts the book down satisfied and picks the next one up curious. Those are different feelings and you want both.

A useful check before you write a book's final chapter. Write down the want you established in its first fifty pages. Then write down what state it is in on the last page. If the answer is "unchanged, but now there are dragons", the book has not ended. It has stopped.

Keeping continuity honest

Continuity across a series is mostly an organisational problem, and it concentrates in one place: time.

The errors readers actually catch are overwhelmingly temporal. Someone is thirty-four in book one and forty in book two, three years later. A war was twelve years ago and then it was twenty. A character remembers an event they were not present at. Physical descriptions drift too, but readers forgive those far more readily than they forgive arithmetic.

Three habits handle most of it. Split the bible, with series-level facts (world, rules, long timeline, recurring cast) separate from per-book facts. Without the split, book three's reference document carries everything and becomes too slow to use, which means you stop using it.

Keep a dated series timeline covering backstory and on-page events together. This is the highest-value page in any multi-book bible.

Do a continuity pass between books, not at the end. Before drafting book two, reread book one with the bible open and correct the bible where the book won. The published text is the truth; your notes are only a record of intentions.

Four ways first series go wrong

Writing three books before finishing one. Tempting, and risky. A structural weakness you have not found yet gets repeated three times, and revising a trilogy is harder than revising a novel by considerably more than a factor of three. Finish book one properly. Outline book two. Know the ending.

Front-loading the world. Book one spends its first third explaining a world the reader has no reason to care about yet. Worldbuilding is earned by story, not the other way round. Start with someone wanting something.

Saving the good material. Writers hold back their strongest ideas for book three. Readers do not reach book three unless book one is the best thing you have. Spend it. You will have more.

Planning at scene resolution. A detailed forty-page outline for book three, written before book one is drafted, will be obsolete and you will still feel obliged to honour it. Plan the shape. Let the scenes wait until the book in front of you is finished.

If you have not yet outlined the first book, start with the outlining guide, and give your recurring cast proper character profiles before book two, since they are the elements that have to carry the most weight over time.

Questions writers ask

How much of a series should I plan before writing book one?
Enough to know the series question, roughly how it resolves, and what book one must plant for later books to use. Detailed plans for books two and three are usually wasted, because writing book one will change them. Plan the shape, not the scenes.
Should each book in a series have its own ending?
Yes, in almost every case. Each book should resolve its own central story while leaving the series question open. A book that ends with nothing settled asks readers to trust you with several more years of their attention before you have earned it.
How long should a book series be?
Trilogies are common because three books map cleanly onto a beginning, a complication, and a resolution. For a first series, three is a sensible ceiling. Open-ended series work best when the books are episodic and each entry is genuinely standalone.
Should I write the whole series before publishing the first book?
Rarely. Writing three full books before finishing and testing one risks repeating a structural mistake three times. A common middle path is to complete book one, outline book two, and know how the series ends.
How do I keep continuity straight across a series?
Split your story bible into series-level facts and per-book facts, and keep a dated timeline covering both backstory and on-page events. Most continuity errors readers notice are temporal: ages, intervals, and how long ago something happened.

Room for more than one book

A series outgrows a single document quickly. By book two you are holding two manuscripts, one shared world, a cast that spans both, and a timeline that has to agree with itself.
