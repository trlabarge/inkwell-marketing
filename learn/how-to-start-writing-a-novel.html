How to Start Writing a Novel

From the idea you keep circling to a first scene on the page, in about a week.

10 min read

The short answer

To start writing a novel, write your idea as one sentence: a character, what they want, and what stands in the way. Sketch a rough ending and your first three scenes, choose a small daily target (300 to 500 words is plenty), and write the first scene this week without editing. Revision comes once the draft is done.

What are the first steps to writing a novel?

Starting a novel takes less preparation than most first-time writers expect. You need a direction and a first scene, not a finished plan.

These seven steps take you from an idea to a draft in progress. The first four fit in a single evening.

  1. Write your idea as one sentence. Name a character, what they want, and what stands in the way. If it will not fit in one sentence yet, that is the first thing to work on, and it takes an evening, not a month.
  2. Decide roughly how it ends. Not the last line, just the shape: does your character get what they want, and what does it cost? An ending gives every session something to write toward.
  3. Meet your main characters. Give each of your two or three main characters a name and one line on what they want. Everything else can wait until the draft asks for it.
  4. List your first three scenes. One line each, with enough detail that you could sit down tomorrow and write the first one without making a major decision.
  5. Choose a small daily target. 300 to 500 words on the days you write is enough to build a habit and finish a draft. Pick the days and the time before you begin.
  6. Write the first scene. Start where something changes for your character, write forward without rereading, and stop when you reach your target.
  7. Keep going without editing. Leave notes in square brackets for anything you want to fix, and keep moving. Revision starts once the draft has an ending.

The order matters less than the momentum. If a scene is already playing in your head, write it tonight and do the first four steps tomorrow. Nothing on this list is a gate you have to pass before you are allowed to write.

How do you turn an idea into a story?

Most novels begin as a fragment: an image, a place, a "what if", a character you cannot stop thinking about. A fragment is not a story yet, because nothing in it is moving. What turns it into one is a character who wants something and a reason they cannot simply have it.

Writers call that sentence the premise. A simple pattern for writing one:

When something happens, a character must do something, or else they lose something, but something stands in the way.

Here is one idea growing into a premise, a piece at a time:

Stage What you have
The fragment An old lighthouse on a cold coast.
Add a character The keeper's daughter, who grew up in the tower.
Add a want She wants to keep the light burning after her father disappears at sea.
Add an obstacle The harbor board will close the lighthouse at the end of the season unless a licensed keeper takes over, and she is too young to hold the license.
Add the stakes If it closes, she loses her home and the last place her father might come back to.
The premise When her father disappears at sea, a lighthouse keeper's daughter must keep the light burning through the season to save her home, but the harbor board will close the tower unless a licensed keeper takes over, and she is too young to qualify.

That sentence is not elegant, and it does not need to be. It already tells you who the story follows, what the middle is about (her attempts to keep the light, and the board's pressure to put it out), and what the ending has to answer. If your own sentence is missing the want or the obstacle, the draft will usually stall right where that piece should be.

Do you need an outline before you start?

No, but you need more than nothing. Writers sit on a spectrum. Plotters want the scenes in order before they begin. Discovery writers find the story by drafting it. Both approaches have produced fine novels, and most writers land somewhere in between.

For a first novel, the useful minimum is small: the premise, a rough ending, and your first three scenes. That is enough to begin each session knowing what to write, without spending months on a plan the draft will change anyway. If you want more structure, the one-sitting outline adds the four turning points in an evening.

Watch for planning that never ends. Building a world, a magic system, or a family tree can feel like progress for months. A useful test: if the next thing you plan will not change the first chapter, it can wait until the draft needs it.

How much do you need to know about your characters?

Less than most character questionnaires suggest. Before you start, you need a name and a want for each main character, and ideally one belief they hold that is not quite true. You will learn the rest by watching them act on the page, which is more reliable than deciding it in advance.

Keep a short note for each character as you write, and add to it whenever the draft invents something: an eye color, a sister, a fear of deep water. The character profile guide covers the handful of fields that actually change how you write a scene.

How many words a day should a beginner write?

Fewer than you think. A small target you hit on most writing days beats a big one you dread. 300 to 500 words is a good place to begin: it fits in under an hour for most people and still adds up to a full draft.

Here is the arithmetic for an 80,000 word novel, the middle of the 70,000 to 100,000 word range most adult novels fall in (see how long a novel should be), writing five days a week:

Words a writing day Time at 750 words an hour Writing days for 80,000 words Weeks at 5 days a week
250 20 min 320 64
500 40 min 160 32
750 1 hr 107 About 22
1,000 1 hr 20 min 80 16
1,500 2 hr 54 About 11

The time column assumes a drafting speed of 750 words an hour, which is a placeholder, not a rule. Time a fifteen-minute session and multiply by four to find your own. To try other targets or work back from a deadline, use the writing pace calculator.

Pick the days and the time as well as the number. "I'll write when I can" tends to become never. "Weekday mornings, 7:00 to 7:40, before anyone else is up" tends to become a draft.

How do you write the first page?

Start where something changes. The first scene should catch your main character at the moment their ordinary life is disturbed, or just before it, so the reader has a reason to turn the page. Backstory, history, and the rules of your world can arrive later, a sentence at a time, when the story needs them.

Three openings tend to stall a first draft: a character waking up and getting ready for the day, a prologue of world history, and a page of description before anyone does anything. If your first scene looks like one of these, try starting it a page or two later and notice what you lose. Usually it is nothing.

Above all, do not polish it. Your first chapter is the one you will rewrite most, because you will not fully know what the book is about until you reach the end. Write it well enough to move on, then move on.

A first-week plan

If you want a concrete schedule, here is one. It has you writing prose by day three, with the planning done in short sessions around it.

Day What to do About how long
Day 1 Write the premise sentence. Write a paragraph on how the story ends. 30 to 45 min
Day 2 Name your main characters, with one line on what each wants. List your first three scenes, one line each. 30 to 45 min
Day 3 Write scene one. Stop at your daily target, even mid-scene. Your daily target
Day 4 Write on. Before you start, read only the last paragraph you wrote yesterday. Your daily target
Day 5 Write on. Add anything new the draft invented to your character notes. Your daily target
Day 6 Rest, unless it is one of your writing days. None
Day 7 Write on. Then write one line for each scene so far, so you can see the shape of the story. Your target, plus 10 min

By the end of the week you have a premise, an ending, a small cast, and a few thousand words of draft. That is a novel in progress, not a plan for one.

Why do first novels stall, and how do you keep going?

Most unfinished first novels are not abandoned on page one. They stall a few chapters in, for a handful of familiar reasons:

  • Rewriting chapter one. Polishing the opening feels like work but does not move the story. Leave a note in square brackets, like [she should already know about the letter], and keep drafting forward.
  • Waiting to feel ready. A schedule and a modest target are what produce a draft. The good days tend to arrive once you are already writing, not before.
  • A shinier idea. A new idea often shows up just as the current one gets hard. Write it in a note and go back to the draft. It will still be there when you finish.
  • Not knowing what happens next. Skip to the next scene you can see clearly and leave [something goes wrong here] in the gap. Or add a complication: let a character lie, fail, or arrive at the wrong moment.
  • Comparing a draft to finished books. The novels you love went through many revisions. A first draft is meant to be rough. Its job is to exist, so you have something to revise.

When the middle slows down, a short burst of focus helps. Many writers use a word sprint: fifteen or twenty-five minutes, timer on, no goal but words. If you want a month of momentum, the 50,000 words in a month guide has daily plans and a catch-up table.

Questions writers ask about starting a novel

How do I start writing a novel with no experience?
Start with one sentence describing your story: a character, what they want, and what stands in the way. Then sketch a rough ending and your first three scenes, and write the first scene at a small daily target such as 300 to 500 words. Experience comes from drafting, not from preparing to draft.
How long does it take to write a first novel?
It depends on your pace. An 80,000 word draft at 500 words a writing day, five days a week, takes 32 weeks, a little over seven months. At 1,000 words a writing day it takes 16 weeks. Revising it usually takes as long again, or longer.
Should I start writing at the beginning of my novel?
You do not have to. If a later scene is vivid and the opening is not, write the vivid scene first. Many writers draft out of order and connect the pieces afterward, and you will probably rewrite the opening once the draft is finished anyway.
Do I need to know the ending before I start writing?
It helps to know roughly how it ends: whether your character gets what they want, and what it costs. That gives every scene a direction. The ending can change as you write, and it often does, but a rough destination keeps the middle from wandering.
How long should my first novel be?
Most adult novels run 70,000 to 100,000 words, and genres vary: adult fantasy often runs 90,000 to 120,000, while middle grade runs 30,000 to 55,000. For a first draft, let the story find its natural length and adjust in revision.
Do I have to write every day?
No. Writing on a regular schedule matters more than writing daily. Three to five set days a week, at a target you can reliably hit, is easier to sustain than every day, and it produces a draft just as surely.
