How to Outline a Novel

Six methods, how much to settle before you draft, and what to do when the story stops obeying the plan.

11 min read

The short answer

To outline a novel: 1) decide how it ends, 2) determine the four turning points that carry the story (the opening, the commitment, the low point, the climax), 3) list the scenes that connect them, and 4) give each scene one line describing what changes during that scene. Choose an outlining method that matches how much uncertainty you can tolerate while drafting, and expect to revise the outline as you write.

Why most outlines fail

An outline usually fails for one of two reasons: Either it is too vague to answer the question you get stuck on, or it is so detailed that writing the book feels like transcription.

Failure #1: The vague outline says things like "they travel to the capital and things get worse." That's a vague idea, and a summary at best. But it's not an outline or a plan. When you sit down with a precious forty-five minutes to write, it tells you nothing about what to write.

Failure #2: The over-specified outline has the opposite problem. It fixes dialogue beats and settings months before you write them, and by the time you arrive, the scene has already been written once in your head. There is nothing left to discover, and drafting becomes a chore.

A useful outline sits somewhere between those, depending on your style. It knows where the story is going and roughly how it gets there, but it leaves the actual scene work to the drafting. The test is simple: open your outline at a random point and ask whether you could start writing from that point right now. If the answer is no, it needs more. If the answer is that you already know every line, it needs less.

A quick reframe. An outline is a hypothesis about your story, not a stone tablet contract. Writers who abandon outlining usually abandoned the stone tablet version. The plan is allowed to be wrong, and it is allowed to change. It is only required to be useful.

Six ways to outline a novel

There is no correct method, only the one that fits how much you need to know before you can write. Here are the six most durable approaches, with what each is good and bad at.

Method What it is Best for Weakness
The beat sheet A fixed list of roughly fifteen story beats (opening image, catalyst, midpoint, dark night, finale) with target word counts for each. Commercial fiction, genre work, and anyone who has stalled in the middle before. Can feel formulaic if you follow it literally rather than treating the beats as questions.
The snowflake Start with one sentence, expand to a paragraph, then a page, then a scene list, growing the story outward in passes. Writers who think top down and want the shape sound before committing detail. The expansion stages can become a way of avoiding the draft.
Index cards One card per scene, physical or digital, rearranged freely until the order feels right. Nonlinear stories, multiple viewpoints, and anyone who thinks better with their hands. Easy to lose the throughline while admiring the arrangement.
The chapter grid A table with a row per chapter and columns for viewpoint, location, time, what changes, and what the reader learns. Multi-viewpoint novels, mysteries, and anything with a timeline to keep honest. Encourages evenness. Not every chapter deserves the same weight.
The reverse outline Draft first, then summarize what you actually wrote, one line per scene, after the fact. Revision, and for discovery writers who need structure without planning. Comes too late to save you from a structurally broken first draft.
Guardrails only Fix three or four fixed points (the ending, the midpoint, one or two set pieces) and improvise everything between. Literary fiction, short novels, and experienced writers who trust their instincts. Requires tolerance for uncertainty and usually a heavier revision.

Most working novelists end up with a hybrid. A beat sheet to establish the spine, a chapter grid to keep a multi-viewpoint book honest, and a reverse outline in revision is a common and effective combination. Do not spend long choosing. The method matters much less than finishing one.

How much to outline before you draft

The honest answer is: enough that you are not afraid of tomorrow's writing session, and no more.

In practice that usually means knowing four things before you begin. You should know your ending, at least in outline. You should know what your protagonist wants and what they actually need, because the gap between those is where most of your story lives. You should know the central conflict clearly enough to state it in one sentence. And you should know your first three or four scenes in enough detail to write them without deciding anything major.

Anything past that is preference. Some writers plan all sixty scenes. Some plan eight. What matters is that you stop planning at the point where planning stops reducing your uncertainty and starts postponing the draft. That point is recognizable: you notice you are making things prettier rather than clearer. In other words: you are tinkering, not creating.

If you only do one thing. Decide the ending. Not the last line, but the shape of the resolution: what your protagonist chooses, what it costs, and what state the world is left in. Every structural question in the middle of a book becomes easier once you know what it has to arrive at.

How to outline a novel in one sitting

If you want a working outline in a couple of hours rather than a couple of months, this is the sequence. It produces a loose but usable plan you can start drafting from tomorrow.

1. Write the one-sentence version

Who wants what, what stands in the way, and what it costs them. One sentence, no subclauses if you can help it. This is harder than it sounds and it is worth the twenty minutes. If you cannot write it, your story shape is not settled, and no amount of scene planning will hide that.

2. Name the ending

Write a paragraph describing how the book resolves. You are allowed to change it later. You are not allowed to skip it, because everything that follows is measured against where the story has to arrive.

3. Find the four corners

Fix four points: the opening situation, the turn where your protagonist commits and cannot go back, the low point where the approach they have been using fails completely, and the climax. These four hold the rest of the book in place. Most structural problems in first drafts are one of these four being missing or arriving at the wrong time.

4. Fill the quarters

You now have four segments. Take each in turn and list the three to six scenes that carry the story from one corner to the next. Do not write them well. Write them fast, in fragments. "She lies to her brother about the letter" is a complete entry.

5. Give every scene a job

Go back through the list and write one line per scene naming what changes in it: a decision, a revelation, a relationship shifting, a door closing. If you cannot name what changes, mark the scene. It is either missing something or it is not a scene, and you have just saved yourself three thousand words.

6. Mark what you do not know

Some parts will be blank. Leave them blank and flag them rather than inventing filler to fill the gap. Those flags become your research list, and in practice they are often where the most interesting material arrives, because you are looking for something specific rather than reading generally.

Two hours of that produces something you can draft from. It will be wrong in places. That is expected and it is not a problem.

When the draft leaves the outline behind

Around the thirty percent mark, a character will do something you did not plan, and it will be better than what you planned. This is the outline working, not failing. The book has told you something it could only tell you by being written.

The rule that serves most writers: finish the scene you are in before you decide anything. Do not stop mid-scene to renegotiate the structure, because you cannot judge a divergence properly until you have seen where it goes. Once the scene is done, ask one question. Is this better than the plan, or is it just easier than the plan? Divergences that are better usually open something up. Divergences that are easier usually avoid a scene you were dreading.

If it is better, update the outline forward from that point. Do not rewrite backward yet, and do not go back and fix the earlier chapters. Note what will need changing and keep drafting. If it is merely easier, write the difficult scene anyway. You can always cut it later, and you usually will not want to.

An outline that gets revised three or four times during a draft is behaving exactly as intended. An outline that survives untouched from first page to last usually means one of two things: either you planned with unusual precision, or you stopped listening to the book somewhere around chapter four.

Outlining for discovery writers (aka: The Pantsers)

If planning genuinely kills your interest in a story, you are not obliged to outline in advance. But most discovery writers are not opposed to structure. They are opposed to deciding it early and making it rigid. Three approaches allow you to keep the pleasure of "finding out" while avoiding a structurally broken draft.

Outline one chapter ahead. Before each writing session, spend five minutes noting what the next chapter needs to accomplish. You'll have some direction for tomorrow without committing to something you won't write until 90 days from now.

Keep a running reverse outline. After each session, add one line summarizing what you just wrote. It costs you only 60 seconds, and by the midpoint you have a complete map of the draft, which is exactly what you need when you get lost. It is also the easiest way to catch the small things that slip, like a character's eye color changing or a journey taking two days in one chapter and a week in another, because you can search one page instead of rereading the whole book.

Fix only the ending. Even the most improvisational writers usually benefit from knowing where they are heading. Everything else can stay open.

Five mistakes to avoid

Outlining the plot, but not the goal. A list of events is not a story. If your outline says what happens, but never says what your protagonist is trying to achieve, the draft will feel like nothing more than a sequence of incidents.

Planning the first act five times. The opening is often the most fun to plan, but is also often the least useful. If you have three versions of chapter one and nothing past chapter six, you are avoiding the hard part that will make your story better.

Treating the outline as finished. An outline is a working document. Keep it beside the draft where you will actually update it, not in a folder you haven't opened for months.

Even weighting. Grids and beat sheets tempt you into giving every chapter the same size and importance. Real novels are lumpy. Some chapters are four pages and some are thirty. Sometimes the action rises, and sometimes it falls. The payoff hits harder when it comes on the heels of thoughtful setup.

Confusing an outline with a story bible. The outline is what happens. The story bible is what is true in your story universe. Keeping them separate and current stops both from becoming unwieldy.

Questions writers ask about outlines

How long should a novel outline be?
Anywhere from one page to forty, depending on how you work. A common working range is one to three pages for a loose outline, or roughly one line per scene for a detailed one, which lands around three to six pages for an 80,000 word novel. Length matters far less than whether the outline answers the question you get stuck on.
Should I outline before or after writing the first draft?
Both work. Outlining first tends to produce a faster, more structurally sound draft. Outlining after a first draft, sometimes called a reverse outline, is often more useful for revision because it shows you what you actually wrote instead of what you intended. Many writers do a light outline before and a reverse outline after.
Does outlining ruin spontaneity?
Only if you treat the outline as a contract. An outline is a hypothesis about the story, not a promise. Most writers who feel constrained by outlines are outlining at the wrong resolution: too much detail in the scenes, not enough clarity about the ending.
What should I do when my draft diverges from the outline?
Keep writing to the end of the scene, then decide whether the divergence is better than the plan. If it is, update the outline from that point forward rather than forcing the draft back. An outline you revise three times during a draft is working correctly.

Keeping the outline beside the draft

Outlines stop being useful when they live somewhere you do not open. The version in a separate document, or in a notebook on the other side of the desk, is the version that goes stale by chapter nine.
