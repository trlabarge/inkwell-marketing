How to Write 50,000 Words in a Month

The daily math, five plans that fit different lives, and what to do on the day the plan falls apart.

8 min read

The short answer

To write 50,000 words in a month, write 1,667 words a day for 30 days. If you write only on weekdays, aim for about 2,400 words a writing day. Prepare your ending and first few scenes before day one, draft forward without editing, and when you fall behind, divide the words left by the days left.

How many words a day is 50,000 words in a month?

Fifty thousand divided by thirty is 1,667 words a day. That number is famous, but it assumes you write every single day, and very few months allow that.

A better place to start is the number of days you can actually write. Count them honestly, with work, family, travel, and holidays in view, then divide 50,000 by that number and round up. Here are five common plans worked out for a 30-day month, using November 2026 as the calendar (it starts on a Sunday and has 21 weekdays and 9 weekend days).

Plan Writing days Words per writing day Good for
Every day 30 1,667 The classic pace. Smallest daily number, but no slack at all.
One rest day a week 26 1,924 Most writers. One guilt-free day off, planned rather than stolen.
Weekdays only 21 2,381 Writers who keep weekends for family, or who write on a commute or lunch break.
Front-loaded 30 2,000 for days 1 to 15, then 1,334 Anyone who knows their energy fades. The first half banks 30,000 words.
Weekends only 9 5,556 Writers with demanding weekdays and long, open weekends. Each day is a marathon.

The arithmetic is the same for any month: words divided by writing days. A 31-day month at every day is 1,613. If you want to plug in your own numbers, the writing pace calculator works out the daily count a deadline needs.

Pick the plan before the month starts. A rest day you planned before you began feels like part of the schedule. The same day taken unplanned partway through feels like failure, and that feeling, more than the lost words, is what makes people stop.

How long does 1,667 words take?

It depends on how fast you draft, which is slower than how fast you type. Thinking, rereading the last paragraph, and deciding what happens next all take time. The only reliable way to know your speed is to measure it: set a timer for fifteen minutes, write without stopping, count the words, and multiply by four.

Once you have that hourly figure, the daily time follows:

Your drafting speed Time for 1,667 words Time for 2,381 words
500 words an hour 3 hr 20 min 4 hr 46 min
750 words an hour 2 hr 13 min 3 hr 11 min
1,000 words an hour 1 hr 40 min 2 hr 23 min
1,500 words an hour 1 hr 7 min 1 hr 35 min

If the time in that table looks impossible beside your actual life, it is better to learn that now and change the plan than to discover it once you've already begun. Two shorter sessions often work better than one long one: a morning session before the day intrudes, and an evening session to finish the count.

Word sprints help too. A sprint is a short, timed burst (often 15 or 25 minutes) where the only goal is words on the page. Four 25-minute sprints at 17 words a minute come to 1,700 words, a full day's quota in under two hours of writing. Sprinting with a friend, even over a message thread, adds just enough accountability to keep you from checking the news between paragraphs.

What to prepare before day one

You do not need a full outline to write 50,000 words in a month, but you do need to avoid deciding the big things on the clock. Every session where you sit down and do not know what happens next costs you time before the first useful sentence. A light plan removes most of that.

Before the first of the month, have these ready:

  • The ending, roughly. Not the last line, just the shape: what your protagonist chooses and what it costs. It gives every session a direction to write toward.
  • The first three or four scenes, in enough detail that you could write them tomorrow without making a major decision.
  • Your main characters, with what each one wants. Names, a few physical details, and the relationship between them. A short character profile is plenty.
  • The four turning points, if you can: the opening, the commitment, the low point, the climax. The one-sitting outline gets you there in an evening.
  • Your writing days and times, written on a calendar, with the rest days marked.
  • One place where the draft lives, and one place for notes, so you never lose ten minutes hunting for a file.

Stop there. Planning past that point tends to become a way of not starting, and the month will teach you more about the story than more preparation would.

How to keep going through the middle

The first few days usually run on excitement. The hard part tends to arrive a little way in, when the opening is written, the ending is still far away, and the story no longer feels new. These habits are what get you through it.

Do not edit. Rewriting yesterday's chapter feels like work, but it does not move the count, and it pulls you into judging the draft before it exists. If something needs fixing, leave a note in square brackets, like [fix: she already knows about the letter], and keep moving forward. You can search for every bracket once the draft is done.

Skip what you do not know. If a scene needs research or a decision you are not ready to make, write [battle goes here] and jump to the next scene you can see clearly. Writing out of order is allowed. The words still count.

Stop mid-sentence. End each session in the middle of a scene you know how to continue, ideally mid-sentence. Starting the next day by finishing a sentence is much easier than facing a blank scene.

Keep a one-line log. After each session, write a single line about what you just drafted. By the end of the month you will have a running reverse outline, which is exactly what you need when you lose track of a subplot partway through, and exactly what you will want for revision.

When you are stuck, change something. Add a complication, let a character lie, or move the scene somewhere else. A new problem will give you more words than staring at the old one.

A word about quality. A fast draft is meant to be rough. Its job is to show you what the story is, so you have something real to revise. Writers who aim for polished prose in a 30-day sprint usually stall, because every sentence has to pass a standard the pace cannot support.

What to do when you fall behind

You probably will fall behind at some point. The fix is the same calculation you started with: divide the words left by the days left. Spread the shortfall across the rest of the month instead of trying to make it up in one heroic session, which tends to cost the following day as well.

Here is what that looks like at a few common moments in a 30-day month:

Where you are Words left Days left New daily number
End of day 1, wrote nothing 50,000 29 1,725
End of day 7, wrote on 4 of 7 days at pace (6,668) 43,332 23 1,884
End of day 15, at 15,000 instead of 25,000 35,000 15 2,334
End of day 20, at 20,000 30,000 10 3,000
End of day 25, at 35,000 15,000 5 3,000

Two things stand out. A missed day early on costs very little: on day one it adds 58 words to each remaining day. And the later you fall behind, the steeper the climb. That is the case for front-loading, and for treating the early days as the time to build a cushion rather than the time to settle in.

If the new number is more than you can write, change the goal rather than abandoning it. Whatever you have written is still part of a novel you did not have before. A good fallback is to finish the story instead of the count: if the draft reaches its ending before the count does, it is a complete draft, and that is the more useful thing to have.

Is 50,000 words a novel?

By some definitions, yes. The Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers Association counts anything from 40,000 words as a novel. But most adult trade novels run 70,000 to 100,000 words, so a 50,000 word draft is usually either a complete short novel or the first half to two thirds of a longer one. Category romance and middle grade sit comfortably near 50,000. Epic fantasy does not. The novel length guide has the ranges by genre.

It helps to decide early which one you are writing. If your story wants to be longer, the month's goal is the first 50,000 words of it, with a plan for the rest once the month ends. If it is a short novel, aim the final stretch at the ending.

Either way, 50,000 words is about 200 double-spaced manuscript pages at 250 words a page (see word count to pages), which is a real stack of paper and a real draft.

Writing 50,000 words in November without NaNoWriMo

National Novel Writing Month made 50,000 words in November a tradition. The nonprofit that ran it closed in 2025, but the challenge itself never needed an organization. It is a target, a deadline, and ideally a few people doing it alongside you.

Writing groups, libraries, and app makers now run their own November challenges, and many writers simply run it themselves. If you are going solo, three things replace most of what the official event offered:

  • A visible count. A daily number you can see, and a running total, so progress is something you can look at rather than a feeling.
  • Company. One or two friends doing the same thing, a shared sprint time, or a weekly check-in.
  • A finish line. Decide now what you will do with the draft the day after the month ends, whether that is rest, reread, or keep going, so the end of the month is a step rather than a cliff.

Nothing about the challenge requires November, either. Any 30 days will do, and a month with fewer obligations is often the better choice.

Questions writers ask about 50,000 word months

How many words a day is 50,000 words in a month?
1,667 words a day in a 30-day month such as November, or 1,613 a day in a 31-day month. If you only write on weekdays, November 2026 has 21 of them, which works out to 2,381 words a writing day.
How long does it take to write 1,667 words?
It depends on your drafting speed. At 1,000 words an hour it takes about 1 hour 40 minutes. At 500 words an hour it takes about 3 hours 20 minutes. Time a fifteen-minute session and multiply by four to find your own hourly rate.
Is 50,000 words a full novel?
It is long enough to count as a novel by the Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers Association's 40,000 word line, but short of the 70,000 to 100,000 words most adult trade novels run. Treat 50,000 words as a complete draft of a short novel, or as half to two thirds of a longer one.
What should I do if I miss a day?
Do not try to make it all up tomorrow. Divide the words you have left by the days you have left and use that as your new daily number. One missed day early in a 30-day month adds fewer than 60 words to each remaining day.
Should I edit while I write 50,000 words in a month?
Keep editing to a minimum. Rewriting yesterday's chapter feels productive but rarely moves the count, and a month is too short to draft and revise at the same time. Note what needs fixing in brackets and come back to it after the month ends.
Is NaNoWriMo still running?
No. The nonprofit behind National Novel Writing Month closed in 2025. The 50,000 words in November tradition carries on through writing groups, libraries, and writers running the challenge on their own, and you need nothing more than a calendar and a word count to take part.
