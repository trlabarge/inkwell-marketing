Character Profile Template

Three free templates to copy, one for every size of role, with a filled-in example to show how each field earns its place.

10 min read

The short answer

A useful character profile template covers five things: what the character wants, what they need, the false belief steering their choices, how they talk, and how they change by the end. Below are free templates for main, supporting, and minor characters, plus a filled-in example. A main character’s profile fills about half a page.

Which character profile template do you need?

Not every character deserves the same page. Match the template to how much of the book the character carries.

The most common mistake with character templates is using one form for everyone. A 40-question sheet for the woman who sells your hero a train ticket is time stolen from the draft, and a three-line note for your protagonist leaves you guessing in every scene they are in. Scale the profile to the role:

Character Template Length
Protagonist or point-of-view characterMain character profileAbout half a page
AntagonistMain profile plus the antagonist add-onHalf a page to a page
Love interest, mentor, sidekick, rivalSupporting character templateEight lines
Named walk-on with more than one sceneMinor character cardThree lines
Appears onceA name and a continuity note in your story bibleOne line

A typical novel needs three or four full profiles. Everyone else can live on a supporting template or a card, and you can promote a character later if the draft gives them more to do.

What does a filled-in character profile look like?

Here is the same template completed for an invented protagonist: a ferry pilot on a small tidal island whose route is about to be put out to tender. Notice how short most answers are. A profile is a set of instructions for writing scenes, not a biography.

Example: Maren Holt

CHARACTER: Maren Holt (Mar to her brother, Skipper to the regulars)
Role in the story: Protagonist, the only point of view
First appears: Chapter 1, running the last crossing in fog
--------------------------------------------
DRIVE
--------------------------------------------
Wants: To keep the ferry licence when the county
  puts the route out to tender on 1 March.
Needs: To accept that the island can survive
  without her holding every part of it.
The lie: "If I let go of any of it, it all goes."
Where it came from: The winter her father's boat
  did not come back, and she ran the house at fifteen.
Afraid of being seen as: Replaceable.
--------------------------------------------
BEHAVIOR
--------------------------------------------
Under pressure she: Gets calmer and slower, and
  starts giving instructions nobody asked for.
When she lies, she: Gives too much detail.
Unaware habit: Checks the sky before answering.
Contradiction: Keeps a perfect ship's log, but has
  not opened her own post in two months.
--------------------------------------------
VOICE
--------------------------------------------
Sentence shape: Short. Stops mid-sentence when the
  subject turns personal.
Register: Plain. Clipped and formal with officials.
How directly she answers: Answers the practical
  half of any question and ignores the rest.
Words she uses: "Right, then." Measures time in
  tides ("back by low water").
Never says aloud: That she has thought about leaving.
--------------------------------------------
PHYSICAL
--------------------------------------------
1. Burn scar across the back of her left hand
2. Her father's oilskin, patched at both elbows
3. Ties her hair back with whatever is to hand,
   usually a length of cord
Continuity: Left-handed. A head shorter than her
  brother. The ferry is the Gannet, 12 passengers.
--------------------------------------------
ARC
--------------------------------------------
At the start, she: Runs the crossing, the post
  run, and her brother's life.
Her approach fails when: She wins the tender by
  underbidding and cannot crew the winter timetable.
At the end, she: Trains a second pilot and lets
  him take the Christmas crossing alone.
What has NOT changed: She still reads the tide
  table before anything else, every morning.
--------------------------------------------
RELATIONSHIPS
--------------------------------------------
Callum (brother): Wants him to stay on the island /
  hides that she has read his job offer.
Ines Okafor (county officer): Wants Ines to see the
  island as people, not a budget line / hides that
  she respects her.

Why this one works:

  • The want has a date. “1 March” gives every chapter a clock, and a clock produces scenes.
  • The lie explains a bad decision. Underbidding the tender is a mistake, but a reader who knows “if I let go of any of it, it all goes” understands exactly why she makes it.
  • The voice section writes dialogue for you. Answering only the practical half of a question is something you can do in every exchange she has.
  • The arc keeps something. She changes in one specific way and stays herself in another, which is what makes a change feel earned rather than convenient.
  • The relationships carry secrets. “Hides that she has read his job offer” is a scene waiting to happen.

Supporting character template

For the people who share scenes with your protagonist: the love interest, the mentor, the friend, the rival. The aim is to keep them distinct and consistent, and to make sure each one wants something in the scenes they are in.

Supporting character

NAME:
Role, and relationship to the protagonist:
What they want in their scenes:
What they know that the protagonist does not:
One voice marker (a habit of speech):
One repeatable physical detail:
Continuity facts already on the page:
Different at the end? (no, or how):

Filled in for Maren’s brother, that might read: Callum Holt. Younger brother, the only family she has left. Wants her blessing to take a job on the mainland without having to ask for it. Knows their father had tried to sell the ferry the year he died. Turns everything into a joke a beat too early. Wears a wristwatch that does not work. Twenty-nine, a chef, allergic to shellfish. Different at the end: leaves, and is the one who calls.

Minor character card

For named characters who appear in more than one scene but do not drive anything. Three lines keep them consistent without turning them into a project.

Minor character card

NAME / where they appear:
The one detail a reader would notice twice:
Continuity (anything already stated on the page):

Promote, don’t pre-fill. If a minor character starts taking over scenes, move them up to the supporting template at that point. Writing full profiles for characters who might matter is one of the most comfortable ways to avoid the draft.

What extra does an antagonist need?

An antagonist gets the full main character profile, because they need a want, a need, and a lie of their own. Add these fields underneath. Together they turn an obstacle into a second protagonist whose story happens to run against the first.

Antagonist add-on

ANTAGONIST ADD-ON
Why their want is reasonable (to them):
Where they are right:
The line they will not cross:
How they see the protagonist:
What they know, and from which chapter:
If they narrated this book, the first line would be:

“Where they are right” is the field that matters most. In the ferry story, Ines Okafor is right that a single pilot cannot safely run a winter timetable. An antagonist who is partly right makes the reader uneasy, and uneasy readers keep turning pages.

What to add for your genre

The core template works for any novel. Some genres lean on extra facts you will need to look up mid-scene. Add a short section for yours:

Genre Fields to add
FantasyAbilities and what each one costs; allegiance or faction; what their culture forbids; languages spoken
Science fictionTechnology they depend on; where they were born and what that means where they live now; rank or status
RomanceWhat they believe they deserve in love; what the other lead sees in them that they cannot; the obstacle inside them, as distinct from the one outside
Mystery and crimeTheir secret; their alibi and who can confirm it; what they know; what they only think they know
HistoricalBirth year and age at each key date; class and what it allows them; period-specific speech you have decided to use or avoid
SeriesState at the end of each book; what the reader knows about them versus what other characters know

For a series, the continuity fields grow quickly. The series planning guide covers what to track across books.

How do you fill in a profile without stalling?

Blank templates invite perfectionism. Fill in the fields that shape a scene first, in this order, then stop:

  1. Write the want with a deadline. One concrete thing they are chasing, with a date or event attached.
  2. Write the lie in their own words. One sentence they believe that is not true, phrased the way they would say it.
  3. Write one ordinary line of dialogue. Have them turn down an invitation. Whatever makes that line theirs goes in the voice section.
  4. Pick three repeatable details. Physical details a reader would notice twice, plus any fact already committed to on the page.
  5. Sketch three states of the arc. Where they start, the moment their usual approach fails, and where they end, including what has not changed.
  6. Stop and draft. Leave every other field blank and fill it in from the page as the draft reveals it.

The rest of the template is a place to put what you discover while writing. Update it in the same sitting you invent something (a sister, a scar, a fear of deep water) so the profile stays true to the draft rather than to your first idea. Questions writers ask about character templates

Is this character profile template free?
Yes. Every template on this page is free to copy and use for any project, with no sign-up. Use the Copy button or select the text, then paste it into whatever you write in: a document, a notebook app, or a folder of index cards.
What should a character profile include?
At minimum: what the character wants, what they need, the false belief that steers their choices, how they speak, two or three repeatable physical details, and how they are different at the end of the story. Everything else is optional and should earn its place by changing how you write a scene.
What is the difference between a character profile and a character bio?
A character bio is usually a short summary of who someone is and where they come from, the kind of thing that might appear on a book’s website. A character profile is a working tool for the writer: it records what the character wants, how they behave under pressure, and how they talk, so you can write them consistently scene after scene.
Should I fill out a profile for every character?
No. A full profile is worth it for the protagonist, the antagonist, and perhaps one or two others. Supporting characters need about eight lines and minor characters three. Characters who appear once need only a name and a continuity note.
Do I need to fill in every field before I start writing?
No. Fill in the want, the lie, a line of voice, three physical details, and a rough arc, then start drafting. Most of a good profile is discovered on the page, so leave the other fields blank and fill them in as the draft reveals them.
