A Glossary of Novel Writing Terms

Plain definitions for the writing craft vocabulary you are likely to come across, and should be able to use.

Story structure Character Point of view and voice Craft and technique Process and publishing

Story structure 15 terms

Inciting incident
The event that disturbs the protagonist's ordinary life and sets the story moving. It usually arrives inside the first ten to fifteen percent of a novel, and it is what makes the rest of the book necessary.
Three-act structure
The most common shape for a novel: a setup that establishes the world and the problem, a longer middle of escalating complications, and a resolution. The act breaks usually fall near the twenty-five and seventy-five percent marks.
Beat sheet
A list of the story's key moments in order, each described in a line or two, used to plan a book or to diagnose one. Beat sheets run from a dozen entries to fifty. See how to outline a novel.
Midpoint
A turn near the center of the book where the protagonist stops reacting and starts acting, or where a revelation changes what the story is about. A sagging middle is very often a midpoint that is not doing enough.
Climax
The scene where the central conflict is decided. Everything before it is preparation, and everything after it is consequence.
Denouement
The material after the climax, where consequences settle and loose threads are tied off. From the French for untying.
Rising action
The stretch between the inciting incident and the climax, where complications accumulate and the stakes climb.
Falling action
The stretch between the climax and the last page. Usually short in a novel, and its job is to let the story come to rest.
Hero's journey
A structural pattern described by Joseph Campbell and adapted for screenwriters by Christopher Vogler, in which a protagonist leaves the familiar world, faces trials, and returns changed. Useful as a lens, restrictive as a template.
Save the Cat
A fifteen beat structure from Blake Snyder's screenwriting book of the same name, widely adapted for novels. Named for the idea that a protagonist should do something early that makes a reader want to follow them.
Freytag's pyramid
A five part structure (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement) described by Gustav Freytag for classical drama. It is where the vocabulary of rising and falling action comes from.
Fichtean curve
A structure built from a series of escalating crises with very little setup, starting close to the trouble. Common in thrillers and in fiction that cannot afford a slow opening.
In medias res
Opening in the middle of the action rather than at the chronological beginning, with the necessary background filled in afterward. Latin for into the middle of things.
All is lost
The low point before the climax, where the protagonist appears to have failed and the goal looks unreachable. Also called the dark night of the soul.
Subplot
A secondary storyline running alongside the main one and usually intersecting it. A subplot that never touches the main plot is often the first thing to cut when a draft runs long.

Character 8 terms

Protagonist
The character whose choices drive the story and whose change the book is about. Not necessarily the narrator, and not necessarily likeable.
Antagonist
The force working against the protagonist. Often a person, sometimes an institution, a condition, or the protagonist themselves.
Character arc
The change a character undergoes across a story, usually in what they believe rather than in what they do. See how to build a character profile.
Flat arc
An arc in which the protagonist does not change but instead changes the world around them. Common in series fiction and in stories with a settled, competent lead.
Want and need
The distinction between what a character is consciously chasing and what they actually require to be whole. Most character arcs are the distance between the two.
The lie
The false belief a character holds about themselves or the world at the start of a story, which the events of the book test and usually break.
Foil
A character who contrasts with another, usually the protagonist, in a way that makes both of them clearer. A foil need not be an opponent.
Stakes
What a character stands to lose. Stakes that are only external, such as the world ending, tend to land less than stakes that are also personal.

Point of view and voice 10 terms

Point of view
The position a story is told from, and whose thoughts the reader is given access to. Often shortened to POV.
First person
A narrator telling their own story as I. Intimate, and limited to what that character knows, notices, and is willing to say.
Third person limited
Told as he, she, or they, but confined to one character's perceptions at a time. The most common point of view in contemporary fiction.
Third person omniscient
A narrator who can see into any character and knows more than all of them. Distinct from head hopping because the narrator is a deliberate, consistent presence.
Second person
A narrator addressing the reader as you. Rare in novels, and striking when it works.
Deep point of view
Third person limited taken to its closest range, where filtering phrases fall away and the prose sits inside the character's head. Not she felt the cold, just the cold.
Head hopping
Switching between characters' interior perspectives inside a single scene without a clear break, usually by accident. It disorients readers even when they cannot name what happened.
Unreliable narrator
A narrator whose account the reader has reason to doubt, whether through self-deception, limited understanding, or intent.
Free indirect discourse
A technique where a character's thoughts and idiom color third person narration without quotation marks or attribution, so the seam between narrator and character dissolves.
Narrative distance
How close the prose sits to a character's consciousness, from a wide external view down to the inside of their thoughts. Strong writing moves along that range on purpose.

Craft and technique 12 terms

Show, don't tell
The advice to dramatize rather than summarize, so the reader draws their own conclusion. Real drafts need both: summary covers ground, scene creates experience.
Exposition
Background information the reader needs in order to follow the story. The craft is in when it arrives and how much weight it carries, not in avoiding it.
Info dump
A block of exposition delivered all at once, usually early, and usually more than a reader can hold. The fix is rarely deletion. It is distribution.
Chekhov's gun
The principle that a detail given significance should pay off, from Chekhov's remark that a rifle hung on the wall in the first act must be fired by the third.
Foreshadowing
Planting a detail early so that a later turn feels inevitable rather than arbitrary. Unlike Chekhov's gun, it need not be explicit or even noticed on a first read.
Subtext
What a scene is about underneath what is being said. Dialogue in which characters state their feelings plainly has none.
Scene and sequel
A rhythm described by Dwight Swain in which a scene (goal, conflict, setback) is followed by a sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision). It explains why unbroken action becomes exhausting to read.
Dialogue tag
The attribution attached to a line of speech: he said, she asked. Said is close to invisible on the page and is usually the right choice.
Action beat
A small gesture or piece of action used in place of a dialogue tag. It attributes the line and does other work at the same time.
Filter words
Verbs that put a layer between the reader and the experience: she saw, he noticed, they realized. Removing them often tightens a paragraph without losing anything.
Purple prose
Prose so ornate that it draws attention to itself rather than to the story it is telling.
MICE quotient
Orson Scott Card's framework naming four story types: milieu, inquiry, character, and event. Its practical use is the reminder that a story should end in the same mode it began.

Process and publishing 14 terms

Plotter and pantser
The two ends of a spectrum of process: writers who plan before drafting, and writers who discover the story as they write. Most people sit somewhere between, sometimes called a plantser.
Discovery writing
Drafting without a detailed plan and finding the story on the page. It usually trades a faster first draft for a longer revision.
Outline
A plan of what happens, in order, at whatever resolution is useful to you. Distinct from a story bible, which records what is true. See how to outline a novel.
Story bible
A reference document holding everything that is true about a book: characters, places, rules, timeline, and terminology. See how to write a story bible.
Reverse outline
An outline written from a finished draft rather than before one, so you can see the shape of what you actually wrote. The fastest structural diagnostic there is.
Zero draft
A draft written purely to find out what the story is, with no expectation that any sentence in it survives.
Beta reader
Someone who responds to a complete draft as a reader rather than an editor, reporting where they were confused, bored, or moved.
Critique partner
Another writer who exchanges work with you, usually at chapter or draft length, and reads with craft in mind rather than as an audience.
NaNoWriMo
National Novel Writing Month, a challenge to write 50,000 words during November, which works out to 1,667 words a day. The organization behind it closed in 2025, but the target survives as shorthand for a fast drafting sprint. See the pace calculator.
Query letter
A one page pitch sent to literary agents: a hook, a short summary, and a line about the writer. It sells the book rather than recounting the plot.
Comp titles
Comparable titles. The recent published books a query names to show where a manuscript would sit on a shelf and who would buy it.
Synopsis
A summary of the whole plot with the ending included, usually one to two pages, written for agents and editors rather than for readers.
Slush pile
The stack of unsolicited submissions waiting to be read at an agency or a publisher.
Manuscript format
The conventions for submitted work: 12 point Times New Roman or Courier, double spaced, one inch margins, and roughly 250 words to a page. See word count to pages.
