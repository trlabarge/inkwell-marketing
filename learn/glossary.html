A Glossary of Novel Writing Terms

Plain definitions for the writing craft vocabulary you are likely to come across, and should be able to use.

124 terms in seven groups, from story structure and character to length, genre, revision, and publishing, each one a word you will meet in the guides, in workshops, and on the way to a finished book.

Story structure 24 terms

Premise
A one-sentence statement of what a story is about: a character, what they want, and what stands in the way. Writing one is usually the first step in starting a novel.
Hook
Whatever in the opening lines makes a reader need to know what happens next: a question, a voice, a situation already in motion. Every chapter ending can carry a smaller one. See how to write the first page.
Inciting incident
The event that disturbs the protagonist's ordinary life and sets the story moving. It usually arrives inside the first ten to fifteen percent of a novel, and it is what makes the rest of the book necessary.
Three-act structure
The most common shape for a novel: a setup that establishes the world and the problem, a longer middle of escalating complications, and a resolution. The act breaks usually fall near the twenty-five and seventy-five percent marks.
Plot point
A turn the protagonist cannot undo, which spins the story in a new direction. In three-act terms, the first plot point ends the setup near the twenty-five percent mark and the second ends the middle near seventy-five percent.
Beat sheet
A list of the story's key moments in order, each described in a line or two, used to plan a book or to diagnose one. Beat sheets run from a dozen entries to fifty. See how to outline a novel.
Midpoint
A turn near the center of the book where the protagonist stops reacting and starts acting, or where a revelation changes what the story is about. A sagging middle is very often a midpoint that is not doing enough.
Pinch point
A scene in the middle of the book that reminds the reader how strong the opposition is, usually one in each half of the second act. Pinch points keep the antagonist present while the protagonist is busy elsewhere.
Climax
The scene where the central conflict is decided. Everything before it is preparation, and everything after it is consequence.
Denouement
The material after the climax, where consequences settle and loose threads are tied off. From the French for untying.
Rising action
The stretch between the inciting incident and the climax, where complications accumulate and the stakes climb.
Falling action
The stretch between the climax and the last page. Usually short in a novel, and its job is to let the story come to rest.
Hero's journey
A structural pattern described by Joseph Campbell and adapted for screenwriters by Christopher Vogler, in which a protagonist leaves the familiar world, faces trials, and returns changed. Useful as a lens, restrictive as a template.
Save the Cat
A fifteen beat structure from Blake Snyder's screenwriting book of the same name, widely adapted for novels. Named for the idea that a protagonist should do something early that makes a reader want to follow them.
Freytag's pyramid
A five part structure (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement) described by Gustav Freytag for classical drama. It is where the vocabulary of rising and falling action comes from.
Fichtean curve
A structure built from a series of escalating crises with very little setup, starting close to the trouble. Common in thrillers and in fiction that cannot afford a slow opening.
In medias res
Opening in the middle of the action rather than at the chronological beginning, with the necessary background filled in afterward. Latin for into the middle of things.
All is lost
The low point before the climax, where the protagonist appears to have failed and the goal looks unreachable. Also called the dark night of the soul.
Subplot
A secondary storyline running alongside the main one and usually intersecting it. A subplot that never touches the main plot is often the first thing to cut when a draft runs long.
Plot twist
A development that overturns what the reader believed was happening. A good twist is surprising on the first reading and inevitable on the second, because the clues were on the page all along.
Flashback
A scene set earlier than the main timeline and dramatized rather than summarized. Useful when the past changes how the present reads, costly when it halts a story that has only just started moving.
Prologue
Opening material set apart from chapter one, often at another time, place, or viewpoint. Prologues divide readers and agents, so one has to give the reader something chapter one cannot.
Epilogue
A closing section set after the main story, often after a jump in time, that shows where the characters landed. Common in romance and in series fiction, where it can point toward the next book.
Frame story
A story that contains another, such as a narrator recounting events years after they happened. The outer story can comment on the inner one. Also called a frame narrative.

Character 16 terms

Protagonist
The character whose choices drive the story and whose change the book is about. Not necessarily the narrator, and not necessarily likeable.
Antagonist
The force working against the protagonist. Often a person, sometimes an institution, a condition, or the protagonist themselves.
Deuteragonist
The second most important character, often a friend, rival, or love interest whose storyline runs beside the protagonist's. From the Greek for second actor.
Character arc
The change a character undergoes across a story, usually in what they believe rather than in what they do. See how to build a character profile.
Flat arc
An arc in which the protagonist does not change but instead changes the world around them. Common in series fiction and in stories with a settled, competent lead.
Want and need
The distinction between what a character is consciously chasing and what they actually require to be whole. Most character arcs are the distance between the two.
The lie
The false belief a character holds about themselves or the world at the start of a story, which the events of the book test and usually break.
The wound
A painful event in a character's past that taught them the lie they believe. Also called the ghost. Knowing it explains why a character keeps acting against their own interest.
Foil
A character who contrasts with another, usually the protagonist, in a way that makes both of them clearer. A foil need not be an opponent.
Round character
A character complex enough to surprise the reader in a convincing way. The term and its opposite come from E. M. Forster's Aspects of the Novel (1927).
Flat character
A character built around a single idea or trait, who behaves the same way each time they appear. Fine in a minor role, a problem in a lead. Not the same thing as a flat arc.
Stakes
What a character stands to lose. Stakes that are only external, such as the world ending, tend to land less than stakes that are also personal.
Backstory
Everything that happened to a character before page one. The writer needs to know far more of it than the reader ever sees, and it reads best when it leaks into the present rather than arriving in blocks.
Motivation
The reason a character wants what they want. A scene that feels false is often one where a character acts because the plot needs them to, not because they would.
Archetype
A recurring character pattern, such as the mentor, the trickster, or the reluctant hero, that readers recognize across stories. A starting shape to fill with specifics, not a substitute for them.
Mary Sue
A character, often a stand-in for the author, who is admired by everyone and good at everything without effort or cost. The label comes from 1970s Star Trek fan fiction. The usual fix is a flaw that costs the character something.

Point of view and voice 15 terms

Point of view
The position a story is told from, and whose thoughts the reader is given access to. Often shortened to POV.
First person
A narrator telling their own story as I. Intimate, and limited to what that character knows, notices, and is willing to say.
Third person limited
Told as he, she, or they, but confined to one character's perceptions at a time. The most common point of view in contemporary fiction.
Third person omniscient
A narrator who can see into any character and knows more than all of them. Distinct from head hopping because the narrator is a deliberate, consistent presence.
Second person
A narrator addressing the reader as you. Rare in novels, and striking when it works.
Deep point of view
Third person limited taken to its closest range, where filtering phrases fall away and the prose sits inside the character's head. Not she felt the cold, just the cold.
Head hopping
Switching between characters' interior perspectives inside a single scene without a clear break, usually by accident. It disorients readers even when they cannot name what happened.
Unreliable narrator
A narrator whose account the reader has reason to doubt, whether through self-deception, limited understanding, or intent.
Free indirect discourse
A technique where a character's thoughts and idiom color third person narration without quotation marks or attribution, so the seam between narrator and character dissolves.
Narrative distance
How close the prose sits to a character's consciousness, from a wide external view down to the inside of their thoughts. Strong writing moves along that range on purpose.
Narrator
The one telling the story. In first person the narrator is a character in it. In third person the narrator may stay close to one character or stand apart from all of them.
Voice
The distinctive sound of a piece of writing: its word choice, rhythm, and attitude. The narrator has a voice, and each character should have their own in dialogue. See making characters sound different.
Narrative tense
Whether a story is told in the past tense (she walked) or the present (she walks). Past is the default for most fiction. Present adds immediacy and is common in young adult and literary fiction. Choose one and hold it.
Interior monologue
A character's thoughts set directly on the page, sometimes in italics, sometimes folded into the narration. Too much of it stalls a scene. None of it keeps the reader outside the character.
Epistolary novel
A novel told through documents such as letters, diary entries, emails, or transcripts. Dracula and Frankenstein both use the form.

Craft and technique 25 terms

Show, don't tell
The advice to dramatize rather than summarize, so the reader draws their own conclusion. Real drafts need both: summary covers ground, scene creates experience.
Exposition
Background information the reader needs in order to follow the story. The craft is in when it arrives and how much weight it carries, not in avoiding it.
Info dump
A block of exposition delivered all at once, usually early, and usually more than a reader can hold. The fix is rarely deletion. It is distribution.
Chekhov's gun
The principle that a detail given significance should pay off, from Chekhov's remark that a rifle hung on the wall in the first act must be fired by the third.
Foreshadowing
Planting a detail early so that a later turn feels inevitable rather than arbitrary. Unlike Chekhov's gun, it need not be explicit or even noticed on a first read.
Subtext
What a scene is about underneath what is being said. Dialogue in which characters state their feelings plainly has none.
Scene and sequel
A rhythm described by Dwight Swain in which a scene (goal, conflict, setback) is followed by a sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision). It explains why unbroken action becomes exhausting to read.
Cliffhanger
A chapter or scene that ends at a moment of unresolved danger or revelation, so the reader has to turn the page to find out what happens. Softer versions end on an open question instead. See where to end a chapter.
Scene break
A visible gap inside a chapter, often a blank line or a centered symbol, that marks a jump in time, place, or viewpoint without starting a new chapter. See how long a chapter should be.
Dialogue tag
The attribution attached to a line of speech: he said, she asked. Said is close to invisible on the page and is usually the right choice.
Action beat
A small gesture or piece of action used in place of a dialogue tag. It attributes the line and does other work at the same time.
Filter words
Verbs that put a layer between the reader and the experience: she saw, he noticed, they realized. Removing them often tightens a paragraph without losing anything.
Purple prose
Prose so ornate that it draws attention to itself rather than to the story it is telling.
MICE quotient
Orson Scott Card's framework naming four story types: milieu, inquiry, character, and event. Its practical use is the reminder that a story should end in the same mode it began.
Theme
What a story is about beneath its plot: the question or idea its events keep testing. Theme often becomes clear only in revision, and it works best when no one states it outright.
Motif
A recurring image, object, or phrase that gathers meaning each time it returns. A motif is concrete. The theme it points to is abstract.
Tone
The attitude writing takes toward its material: wry, tender, menacing, detached. Tone is set by voice and word choice, and a sudden shift reads as a mistake unless it is clearly deliberate.
Tension
The reader's unease about what will happen next. It comes from unanswered questions and unresolved conflict, and it can live in a quiet conversation as easily as in a chase.
Pacing
How fast a story feels, set by scene and sentence length and by how much happens on the page compared with how much is summarized. See varying chapter length.
Red herring
A false clue that points the reader toward the wrong conclusion. Essential in mystery, and fair only when the true answer was also on the page.
MacGuffin
An object everyone in the story wants, which matters for the chase it causes rather than for what it is. Alfred Hitchcock made the term famous.
Deus ex machina
An ending in which an unforeseen outside force solves the protagonist's problem for them. Latin for god from the machine, after the crane that lowered gods onto the stage in Greek theatre. Readers feel cheated because the win was not earned.
Plot hole
A gap or contradiction in a story's logic, such as a character knowing something they could not know. Many creep in during revision, when a scene changes and the scenes that depended on it do not.
Worldbuilding
Inventing the setting a story needs: its places, history, rules, and cultures. The reader sees a fraction of it, and keeping the rest consistent is what a story bible is for.
Magic system
The rules that govern magic in a fantasy world: who can use it, what it costs, and what it cannot do. The clearer the limits, the more a reader trusts magic that solves a problem.

Length and genre 13 terms

Flash fiction
A complete story in under 1,000 words. Some markets draw the line lower, and a drabble is exactly 100 words.
Short story
Fiction under 7,500 words, by the Nebula Award rules that the rest of publishing has largely adopted.
Novelette
Fiction from 7,500 to 17,500 words by the Nebula Award rules. The category is used mostly by science fiction and fantasy magazines and awards.
Novella
Fiction from 17,500 to 40,000 words by the Nebula Award rules. Long enough for a full arc, short enough to read in a sitting or two.
Novel
Book-length fiction: 40,000 words or more by the Nebula Award rules, though most adult novels run 70,000 to 100,000. See how long is a novel.
Genre
The category a book is shelved and sold in, such as fantasy, romance, mystery, or thriller. Genre sets what readers expect of length, pacing, and above all the ending.
Subgenre
A narrower category inside a genre, such as cozy mystery, epic fantasy, or romantasy. Length expectations are often set at this level. See novel word counts by genre.
Trope
A story pattern readers know by name, such as enemies to lovers or the chosen one. Readers often seek out the tropes they love, and the craft is in what a writer does with them.
Literary fiction
Fiction valued mainly for its prose, its characters' inner lives, and its ideas rather than for genre conventions. It is a shelf as much as a style.
Commercial fiction
Fiction written for broad appeal, driven by plot and built around genre expectations. The opposite pole to literary fiction, with upmarket fiction in between.
Upmarket fiction
Fiction between literary and commercial: accessible and plot-driven, with prose and themes that invite book club discussion. A label used mostly by agents and in query letters.
Young adult
Fiction for readers of roughly 12 to 18, usually with teenage protagonists. Typical lengths run 55,000 to 80,000 words. See novel word counts by genre.
Middle grade
Fiction for readers of roughly 8 to 12. Typical lengths run 30,000 to 55,000 words. See novel word counts by genre.

Process and revision 17 terms

Plotter and pantser
The two ends of a spectrum of process: writers who plan before drafting, and writers who discover the story as they write. Most people sit somewhere between, sometimes called a plantser.
Discovery writing
Drafting without a detailed plan and finding the story on the page. It usually trades a faster first draft for a longer revision.
Outline
A plan of what happens, in order, at whatever resolution is useful to you. Distinct from a story bible, which records what is true. See how to outline a novel.
Story bible
A reference document holding everything that is true about a book: characters, places, rules, timeline, and terminology. See how to write a story bible.
Reverse outline
An outline written from a finished draft rather than before one, so you can see the shape of what you actually wrote. The fastest structural diagnostic there is.
Zero draft
A draft written purely to find out what the story is, with no expectation that any sentence in it survives.
Beta reader
Someone who responds to a complete draft as a reader rather than an editor, reporting where they were confused, bored, or moved.
Critique partner
Another writer who exchanges work with you, usually at chapter or draft length, and reads with craft in mind rather than as an audience.
NaNoWriMo
National Novel Writing Month, a challenge to write 50,000 words during November, which works out to 1,667 words a day. The organization behind it closed in 2025, but the target survives as shorthand for a fast drafting sprint. See how to write 50,000 words in a month, and the pace calculator.
Word sprint
A short, timed burst of drafting, usually 15 to 25 minutes, where the only goal is getting words on the page. Often done alongside other writers. See how sprints add up to a daily count.
Revision
Changing a draft at the level of story: cutting and adding scenes, moving chapters, reshaping arcs. It usually comes before editing, which works on sentences.
Kill your darlings
Revision advice to cut passages you love when they do not serve the book. Often credited to Faulkner, it traces back to Arthur Quiller-Couch, who told students to murder their darlings in a 1914 lecture.
Developmental edit
An editor's read of the whole book for structure, character, pacing, and plot, delivered as a letter and notes in the margin. The first stage of professional editing. Also called a structural edit.
Line edit
Editing sentence by sentence for clarity, rhythm, and voice, once the structure is settled.
Copyedit
Editing for correctness and consistency: grammar, spelling, punctuation, and facts such as a character's eye color staying the same across the book. Usually done to a style guide such as The Chicago Manual of Style.
Proofreading
The last check of typeset pages, for errors that survived the copyedit or crept in during layout.
Writer's block
A stretch in which a writer cannot move the work forward. It is usually a symptom (an unclear next scene, a wrong turn several chapters back, fatigue) rather than a mystery, and naming the cause is the first step. See why first novels stall.

Publishing 14 terms

Query letter
A one page pitch sent to literary agents: a hook, a short summary, and a line about the writer. It sells the book rather than recounting the plot.
Comp titles
Comparable titles. The recent published books a query names to show where a manuscript would sit on a shelf and who would buy it.
Logline
A one or two sentence summary of a story built to sell it: protagonist, goal, obstacle, and stakes. Borrowed from screenwriting, and close kin to the pitch line in a query letter.
Synopsis
A summary of the whole plot with the ending included, usually one to two pages, written for agents and editors rather than for readers.
Slush pile
The stack of unsolicited submissions waiting to be read at an agency or a publisher.
Literary agent
A professional who sells an author's book to publishers and negotiates the contract in exchange for a commission, commonly 15 percent of domestic earnings. Most large publishers accept novels only through agents.
Traditional publishing
A publisher buys the rights to a book, pays the author an advance, and covers editing, design, printing, and distribution. Usually reached through a literary agent.
Self-publishing
Publishing a book yourself through retailers and distributors such as Amazon KDP, IngramSpark, or Kobo. The author keeps the rights and a larger share of each sale, and pays for editing and design up front.
Advance
Money a traditional publisher pays an author before publication, against future royalties. The author keeps it even if the book never earns it back, but sees no royalties until it does.
Royalty
The author's share of each sale, set in the contract as a percentage of the list price or of the publisher's net receipts.
ARC
Advance reader copy: an edition sent before publication to reviewers, booksellers, and early readers to build word of mouth.
Front matter
Everything before chapter one in a published book: title page, copyright page, dedication, and table of contents.
Back matter
Everything after the story ends: acknowledgments, an author's note, the author biography, and often a preview of the next book.
Manuscript format
The conventions for submitted work: 12 point Times New Roman or Courier, double spaced, one inch margins, and roughly 250 words to a page. See word count to pages.
