How to Build a Character Profile

Why long questionnaires produce flat characters, and the handful of fields that genuinely change how a scene gets written.

8 min read

The short answer

Skip the long questionnaires. A useful character profile answers three questions: what does this character want, what do they believe that is not true, and how do they sound when they speak? Everything else is optional. A field earns its place only if it changes how you write a sentence.

Why long questionnaires produce flat characters

You can know a character's blood type, childhood pet, and favorite season, and still have no idea what they would do if someone lied to their face.

That is the problem with the hundred-question character sheet. It gathers facts, but facts are not behavior. A character comes alive on the page when the reader is invested in (and can relate to) how they will respond to pressure and then watch them do something slightly different. Not many of the standard questionnaire fields help with that, because few of them are about pressure.

There is a second, more understated problem, as well. Filling in a long profile feels productive and costs very little emotionally, which makes it an excellent way to avoid actually writing your draft. Writers who have completed detailed sheets for a cast of characters and drafted only a chapter or two have not been lazy. However, they have been busy in the wrong place.

The test for any field in a profile: will it make you change the way you write a sentence? If knowing a character's favorite season has never altered a line of prose, then it is decoration. Some decoration is fine, and often fun. Pages of this type of decoration should be a warning that you're likely going deeper than is needed.

Where questionnaires do work. Use them as a rescue tool, not a starting point. When a character will not come alive multiple chapters in, working through a list of odd questions can help shake something loose. The difference is that at that point you are answering questions about someone you have watched act, rather than inventing a stranger.

The fields that change your prose

Here is what actually earns its place, and what each field does when you are stuck mid-scene.

Field What it answers When you use it
Want What they are consciously chasing, stated concretely. Every scene. It tells you what they are trying to get out of the conversation.
Need What they actually require to be whole, which they cannot see yet. Structuring the arc, and deciding what the ending costs them.
The lie The false belief they hold about themselves or the world. Whenever they make a decision that needs to be wrong but understandable.
Wound The past event that installed the lie. Writing backstory, and knowing which subjects they deflect.
Voice Sentence length, register, deflections, what they never say. Every line of dialogue. The most-used field in practice.
Under pressure What they do when frightened, cornered, or humiliated. Every scene with conflict in it, which should be most of them.
Physical markers Two or three specific, repeatable details. Description, and keeping continuity honest across a long draft.
Contradiction One thing about them that does not fit the rest. Whenever they threaten to become a type rather than a person.
Arc Where they start, when their approach fails, where they end. Revision, and any scene you suspect is standing still.

Start with these nine fields. For a main character, that is about half a page. These nine fields will do more work for your characters than many pages of biography.

Want, need, and the lie between them

Most troubles your characters will experience is "want troubles." If a character feels passive, or scenes feel like people exchanging information, the usual cause is that nobody in the room is trying to get anything. Remember Kurt Vonnegut's guidance: "Every character should want something, even if it is only a glass of water."

The want should be concrete and external. "To be happy" is not a want, because you cannot put it in a scene. "To get her brother's name off the lease before Friday" is a want, because it produces action, obstacles, and a deadline. Vague wants produce vague scenes and vague characters.

The need is internal and oftentimes opposed to the want. She wants the lease sorted. She needs to stop managing her family's lives as a way of avoiding her own. The story is the machine through which all of this can take place. When people say a novel has no emotional depth, they usually mean the need is missing. They cannot feel the internal pressure of the characters, which means they cannot relate to them, and as a result they do not care what happens to them.

The lie is often what creates the tension. It is the sentence the character believes that is not true: "if I stop holding this together, everyone leaves." The lie explains why an intelligent person keeps choosing badly, which is the single hardest thing to make convincing in fiction. If you know the lie, bad decisions write themselves and the reader forgives them.

The wound is where the lie came from. Useful, but less useful than writers expect, and it does not need to be dramatic. A great deal of backstory in first drafts is trauma invented to justify a lie that was already working fine on its own.

Voice: making characters sound different

The quickest diagnostic in fiction: remove the dialogue tags on a page and see whether you can still tell who is speaking. Most first drafts fail this, because every character talks the way the author talks.

Voice is not an accent or catchphrase. It is a relationship to language, and four variables carry almost all of it.

Length and shape. Some people speak in complete paragraphs, some in fragments. Some circle back and qualify. Some stop before the sentence is done.

Register. Formal or colloquial, technical or plain, and whether it shifts by audience. A character whose register changes when their mother walks in has just been characterized in a single line.

Directness. How close to the actual answer do they get when asked a question. Evasion is the most reliable voice marker there is, because it works even in short exchanges.

What they will not say. This has the capability of being the most powerful, but is often the most neglected. Every character has a subject they route around. Knowing which route that is lets you write silence, and silence is ironically where a lot of good dialogue lives.

A five minute exercise. Write one plain line of dialogue that any of your characters might have to say. Something with no drama in it at all, like turning down an invitation. Then write how each of your main characters would actually say it.

One says "I can't, sorry." One says "Oh, that sounds lovely, let me see what I can move around." One says nothing about the invitation and asks who else is going. Same refusal, three people. Whatever made those versions different is that character's voice, and you can now write it down in a line or two and use it in every scene they appear in.

Physical detail that does work

Full physical descriptions are largely inert. Readers construct faces regardless of what you tell them, and a paragraph of hair and eyes and height rarely survives past the chapter it appears in. Not that you shouldn't do it at all, just know that its impact is often less than authors think.

Two or three specific details do far more, especially when they carry information. Bitten nails on a character who insists she is calm. A jacket a size too large, inherited. Hands that are always doing something. These are characterization disguised as description, and because there are only a few of them, you can repeat them, and repetition is what makes a reader see someone.

Keep the continuity-critical facts in the profile too (and possibly in your story bible), plainly: height relative to other characters, anything visible and permanent, and anything you have already committed to on the page. This is the section that stops a character's eyes changing color later in the story by accident, which sounds like a joke until it happens to you.

Tracking a character as they change

A profile written once, before drafting, describes a character who no longer exists by your third act. The person at the end of a novel should not answer the same questions the same way, and a static profile will become a historical record of someone the book has left behind.

The fix is small and simple. Give the profile three states rather than one.

At the start. What they believe, what they want, how they behave under pressure at the opening of the book.

At the break. The point where their usual approach fails completely. This is usually the low point of their arc and it is the single most useful thing to know in advance, because everything before it is them trying the old way and everything after is them trying something else.

At the end. What has changed, what has not, and what it cost. Note the "what has not" explicitly. Characters who change entirely feel like different people, but characters who change in one specific way feel real.

When you revise, read the three states in sequence. If they read as the same person three times, the arc is not on the page yet, no matter what your outline says.

Profiles for everyone else

Not every character needs this. A novel with forty named people needs three or four full profiles and thirty-six entries of just a few lines.

For supporting characters, one want and one voice marker is usually enough to keep them distinct and consistent. For characters who appear once, a name and a continuity note is plenty. The temptation to profile everybody is strong, particularly when the draft is going badly, but it should be resisted for the same reason as the long questionnaire: it feels like writing and it is not.

Where the effort does pay off is antagonists. An antagonist with a want, a need, and a lie of their own stops being an obstacle and becomes a second protagonist, one whose story simply happens to run against your first. That is where the shades of gray come from: the reader understands them, sometimes agrees with them, and is uneasy about it. It turns a story about beating someone into a story about two people who both have a case, and that difference is felt on every page they appear on. If you profile only two characters in your novel, make them the protagonist and whoever is stopping them.

Questions writers ask

What should a character profile include?
At minimum: what the character wants, what they actually need, the false belief driving their decisions, two or three speech habits, a small number of repeatable physical details, and how they are different at the end of the story. Everything beyond that is optional and should earn its place.
How long should a character profile be?
Half a page to two pages for a main character, and a few lines for anyone else. Long profiles are usually a symptom of avoiding the draft. If a field has never once changed a sentence you wrote, it is decoration.
Are character questionnaires useful?
Rarely in their long form. A hundred-question sheet produces a well-documented character who still feels flat, because trivia does not generate behavior. Questionnaires work best as prompts when a character is not coming alive, not as a form to complete before drafting.
Should I write character profiles before or during the draft?
Write a short profile before, covering want, need, wound, and voice, then let the rest accumulate as you draft. Characters reveal themselves through action, and most of the useful detail in a profile is discovered on the page rather than invented in advance.
How do I make my characters sound different from each other?
Give each one a different relationship to language: sentence length, register, how directly they answer a question, and what they refuse to say. Covering the dialogue tags and reading a page aloud will tell you quickly whether two characters are distinguishable.

Profiles that sit beside your scenes

A character profile is most useful at the moment you are writing the character. In a separate document, it often becomes "a thing you meant to check." When it lives right beside your draft, it is something you can quickly glance at and reference as needed to improve your narrative.
